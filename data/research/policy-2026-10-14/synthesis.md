Robotics policy, as of October 14, 2026

The documents show two different policy priorities. China’s plan connects robotics development to wider industrial adoption. The U.S. records emphasize security restrictions, alongside proposals to coordinate research, production and deployment. This is a reading of the documents, not proof that either country’s programs have delivered their intended results.

China’s National People’s Congress approved the 2026–2030 plan on [March 12, 2026](https://www.bjrd.gov.cn/zyfb/zt/14jqgrd04chybjdbthdztbd/wjbg/202603/t20260313_4556146.html). The [outline](https://www.bjrd.gov.cn/zyfb/zt/14jqgrd04chybjdbthdztbd/wjbg/202603/t20260314_4557219.html) places robotics among strategic emerging industries and embodied intelligence among future industries. It calls for application demonstrations, industrial clusters and AI adoption. That connects technology development with demand and production capacity. It does not establish an official ten-track taxonomy, a robotics-specific appropriation or a quantified national robot target in the passages reviewed.

The U.S. sequence began before 2026: Commerce initiated its [robotics and industrial-machinery Section 232 investigation](https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2025-18749) on September 2, 2025, and Cassidy introduced the [Humanoid ROBOT Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s3275is/html/BILLS-119s3275is.htm) on November 20. The latter proposes federal procurement and contractor restrictions, investment review and threat reporting. The American Security Robotics Act followed in March–April 2026, proposing procurement restrictions and delayed limits on federal operation and spending.

Four hearings involved robotics between March and July. April’s hearing focused on robotics manufacturing and automation; March examined AI, robotics and autonomous technologies. June and July considered broader legislative packages that included robotics bills. These establish congressional attention, not enacted programs or unprecedented hearing frequency.

The [National Commission on Robotics Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr7334ih/html/BILLS-119hr7334ih.htm), introduced in the House in February and Senate in June, proposes a temporary advisory commission covering competitiveness, workforce, manufacturing and supply chains. It differs from SCSP’s nongovernmental commission. Neither a proposed commission nor its future report supplies an implementation budget by itself.

June’s GUARD Act and the Blocking CCP Spy Tech bills propose national-security reviews with FCC listing consequences, including listing when agencies miss deadlines. Separately, the FCC [actually added foreign-produced advanced robotic devices](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-786A1.pdf) to its Covered List on July 28, subject to conditional approvals. This concerns equipment authorization and related restrictions; it is not the Commerce investigation or a general possession ban. The House-passed NDAA’s §163 would restrict DoD procurement, leasing or obtaining covered humanoid systems tied to foreign adversaries, with testing waivers and specified exclusions. H.R.8800 reached the Senate September 14; it is not enacted. Official action histories captured October 14 show GUARD forwarded by subcommittee September 1; the other seven standalone bills remain at referral.

Author policy options — these are proposals, not source recommendations:

- Fund time-limited deployment trials for smaller manufacturers. Measure uptime, cost per task and worker training against a baseline. This addresses adoption directly, but grants can subsidize purchases firms would have made anyway.
- Publish a clear security approval route with device scope, deadlines and renewal rules. This could preserve access to approved equipment while addressing data and remote-control risks; credible testing and continuing oversight cost money.
- Assign an implementation owner and milestones alongside any commission. Reports could then lead to named decisions and accountable budgets. Coordination can also add delay if it duplicates existing agencies.
- Tie component and integration support to demonstrated production bottlenecks and customer orders. This could turn research strengths into manufacturing capacity; broad origin rules or subsidies may raise costs and protect weak suppliers.
