    Physical AI
    Action Plan

    A U.S. robotics agenda built around deployment, industrial capacity, and trusted cooperation. Draft options for decision; no agency has adopted this document.

    Prepared for the Robotics Leadership Tracker · Evidence cutoff: 22 September 2026 · Print: six-page brief

    01 / Outline and framing

    From AI capability to productive machines

    The 2025 AI Action Plan uses short, action-led sections inside three pillars: innovation, infrastructure, and international diplomacy and security. The 2026 National Security Science and Technology Strategy adds focused competition, resilience, speed, protection, and means of implementation. NSTM-5 sets the FY2028 R&D budget frame. Public NSPM-11 covers national-security AI; it is not a general robotics statute.

    Core test. Count field use, worker and safety outcomes, domestic component capacity, and repeat purchases. Do not count a simulation demo, pilot announcement, or factory design target as deployment.
    1. Pillar I — Deploy and learn: independent benchmarks, open and lawful data, small-firm adoption, and a usable safety evidence path.
    2. Pillar II — Build the industrial base: precision components, financing and procurement channels, workforce, and mission test sites.
    3. Pillar III — Work with trusted partners: existing UK, Japan, and Korea deals, interoperable tests and components, plus entity-level security review.
    4. Execution: OSTP convenes; lead agencies use existing authorities or seek explicit new authority. Publish a baseline, pilots, results, and corrections.

    Framing adapts document architecture, not White House text. Proposed steps below require agency, budget, legal, and security review.

    02 / Evidence and blockers

    The gap is adoption and repeatability

    Adoption is uneven

    NSF/Census 2021 business data show autonomous systems and robotics use at 33% among firms with 25,000 or more employees and about 4% among firms with 1–9 employees. This is a broad technology category, not a count of deployed industrial robots. Source.

    Industrial installs recovered

    IFR's preliminary U.S. figure is 38,000 industrial robot installations in 2025, up 11%. It excludes many service and humanoid systems; it does not measure utilization or return on investment. Source.

    Integration and safety cost matter

    OSHA's industrial robotics manual covers collaborative, non-collaborative, and mobile applications and emphasizes site-specific hazard assessment. NIST began a humanoid baseline benchmark in 2026; it is still in development. A recent 300-trial research benchmark probes unsafe-instruction refusal in one setup; it is not a field injury rate. OSHA · NIST.

    Data is fragmented

    Census ASM tracks industrial robot presence, purchases, and capital spending. Open robot-learning sets such as DROID, Open X-Embodiment, and LeRobot show usable formats, but coverage, rights, and transfer across hardware vary.

    Watch items. Commerce's robotics and industrial-machinery Section 232 investigation is listed as an investigation; an investigation is not a tariff. The FCC's July 2026 Covered List addition for foreign-produced advanced robotic devices has a DoW conditional-approval route. These create sourcing and authorization questions, but their precise effect depends on device and supply chain. The 2025 Census baseline and import classifications need current, product-specific review before estimating market size or tariff exposure.

    03 / Pillar I — Accelerate useful deployment

    Make real-world performance legible

    1. Common field-test evidence

    Lead option: NIST · partners: OSHA, NSF, employers, unions, insurers, standards bodies

    Extend NIST's in-development humanoid work into voluntary, task-level test methods for manipulation, mobile navigation, human proximity, recovery after fault, and maintenance. Publish protocols, failure definitions, and independent results; report task success and incidents separately. OSHA's current hazard-assessment guidance remains the workplace floor. A voluntary test method would not replace legal compliance or certify every worksite. NIST · OSHA.

    Decision gate: scope to one or two repeatable tasks first; measure inter-lab agreement before wider use.

    2. Data commons with rights and provenance

    Lead option: NSF and NIST · partners: universities, labs, nonprofits, vendors, states

    Use open calls for diverse, consented robot task data and failure cases, with documented licenses, sensors, embodiment, site conditions, human review, and restrictions. Compare with DROID, Open X-Embodiment, LeRobot, and the recent Eidon first-person dataset. Release evaluation splits separately from training data. Secure sensitive facilities and worker data; open data is conditional on rights and safety.

    Decision gate: publish a data-rights checklist and two independent baseline evaluations before scale-up.

    3. Adoption evidence for smaller plants

    Lead option: NIST MEP · partners: states, community colleges, manufacturers, SBA

    MEP centers already advise small manufacturers on automation. September 2026 awards provide more than $30 million in first-year support to 12 centers for AI, robotics, automation, and additive manufacturing, with at least 50% nonfederal match. Centers can compare automation opportunities with plant-level integration cost, uptime, worker training, and payback. A common, anonymized evaluation template could reveal where robots are uneconomic as well as successful. Link findings to Census ASM robot measures and NSF/Census business-use data; publish methods that protect confidential firms. Existing 100% bonus depreciation may change equipment economics for eligible taxpayers, but is not a grant.

    04 / Pillar II — Build American capacity

    Turn pilots into production capacity

    4. Map bottleneck parts before funding them

    Lead option: Commerce/NIST · partners: DOE, DoD, OEMs, tier suppliers, state MEP centers

    Commission a supplier-level map of precision reducers, bearings, roller screws, frameless motors, power electronics, sensors, and advanced magnets: domestic output, qualifying alternatives, lead times, cost, and single-source risk. The dashboard's supply-chain tab identifies these as hypotheses for verification, not a complete national capacity inventory. Use the map to test whether existing manufacturing, procurement, and R&D channels address demonstrated gaps. Do not infer a product's origin from a supplier headquarters.

    Decision gate: publish methodology and aggregate findings; keep firm-sensitive data protected.

    5. Mission sites for hard tasks

    Lead options: DOE, USDA, and federal facility owners · partners: labs, states, firms, universities

    Run bounded demonstrations where public missions create test value: hazardous inspection, laboratory automation, agricultural handling, and facility logistics. DOE's Genesis consortium already names robotics and automation as a working-group area; the NSF-backed UT Austin center studies human–robot work. Each site would define an operator, safety case, data rights, independent evaluation, and post-pilot purchasing path. A demonstration without a repeat buyer is a learning result, not commercial adoption.

    6. State finance and worker pathways

    Lead options: state economic-development agencies · partners: DOL, community colleges, MEP, employers

    Use verified existing programs, with eligibility shown at point of use: Texas Enterprise Fund for large competing sites; Michigan's general R&D credit; New York Excelsior with job thresholds; Pennsylvania Innovate in PA 2.0; and California Competes. A separate training track could measure technician placement, safety skills, wages, and retention rather than “jobs promised.”

    05 / Pillar III — Trusted international cooperation

    Use deals already signed

    Three 2025 U.S. Technology Prosperity Deals create existing channels. A country-level agreement does not establish that a particular firm, dataset, component, or investor has no China, Russia, or North Korea nexus. Any project requires entity, beneficial-owner, supply-chain, export-control, and data-access review.

    7. Interoperable, secure evaluation

    Lead option: OSTP/State/Commerce · partners: UK, Japan, Korea agencies and standards bodies

    Start with shared vocabulary for task success, failure, safety incident, component provenance, and data rights. Permit reciprocal tests only where export controls, research security, and agency authorities allow. The NSSTS calls for deeper work with allies while protecting research; the FCC robot Covered List shows why device-specific review matters.

    06 / Delivery and accountability

    A sequence that can be audited

    PeriodConcrete output for decisionFailure signal
    0–90 daysOSTP inventory of agency authorities, live robotics programs, and overlaps; NIST benchmark scoping; Commerce component-map specification; legal review of data sharing.No named owner, no baseline, or a proposed activity outside available authority.
    3–12 monthsPublic test methods for limited tasks; two independent datasets or evaluation splits; MEP adoption templates; first supplier map; small number of mission-site trials.Results cannot be reproduced; vendors report only demos, not uptime, incidents, cost, and operator burden.
    12–24 monthsIndependent field evaluation, repeat procurement where earned, workforce outcomes, supplier diversification evidence, and a public update of this plan.No repeat buyer; safety or worker outcomes omitted; domestic-content claims lack part-level proof.

    Measures. Report task success by environment; uptime and mean repair time; worker injuries and near misses; total integration and operating cost; technician placements and wages; domestic and trusted-partner parts by bill of materials; repeat purchases. Disaggregate by firm size and application. Publish negative results where lawful.

    Authority and budget. Existing NIST, NSF, DOE, Commerce, OSHA, Census, state, and tax programs each have distinct missions. The options above do not presume a new appropriation, mandatory standard, or blanket purchase preference. OSTP and OMB can coordinate a budget proposal under NSTM-5; Congress and agencies decide authority and funding. Procurement and international work remain subject to their own rules.
