    Physical AI
    Action Plan

    Federal options for robotics research, U.S. industrial capacity, deployment at working sites, and rules for safe use.

    Research draft · 22 September 2026 · Prepared for the Robotics Leadership Tracker

    Four pillars for U.S. robotics

    The 2025 AI Action Plan organizes agency actions by pillar. The 2026 National Security Science and Technology Strategy asks for faster research, stronger production, and work with allies. Science: A New Golden Age (NSTM-5) sets the FY2028 federal R&D budget frame. NSPM-11 addresses AI used for national security. Civilian robotics actions below need their own agency authority and funding.

    The market case is specific. NSF/Census data show autonomous-systems and robotics use in 33% of firms with at least 25,000 employees, versus about 4% of firms with 1–9 employees; that survey category is broader than industrial robots. IFR reports a preliminary 38,000 U.S. industrial-robot installations in 2025, up 11%; installations say little about uptime or payback. NSF/Census · IFR.

    The Foundation for American Innovation (FAI) estimates that Japanese- and European-headquartered firms earn about 85% of traditional industrial-robot revenue. Its analysis also finds faster Chinese growth in collaborative robots and substantial U.S. reliance on imported arms. These are FAI estimates. U.S. agencies could test American AI controls on screened allied arms while qualifying domestic parts and local installers.

    1. R&D: task tests, usable data, and work on variable industrial tasks.
    2. Industrial base: part-level capacity, local integration, and trusted suppliers.
    3. Deployment at scale: repeat purchases, smaller manufacturers, and trained workers.
    4. Regulatory policies: clear safety evidence, defined operators, and product-specific security review.

    Measure: robots operating at customer sites, task completion, uptime, incidents, integration cost, worker outcomes, and repeat orders. Report pilot announcements and planned factory capacity separately.

    1. Research and development

    Test tasks that factories need

    Lead: NIST and NSF. Work with: DOE labs, universities, unions, manufacturers, and standards bodies.

    Expand NIST's developing robot benchmark beyond a single body shape. Start with two tasks where fixed programming struggles: handling variable parts and recovering safely after a fault. Record completion, cycle time, intervention, energy use, and failures across different arms, grippers, and mobile bases. FAI's market analysis treats cheaper, software-intensive cobots as a possible indicator of future AI-integrated industrial arms; a shared test would check that claim against real work.

    First decision: NIST publishes task definitions and an inter-lab comparison before any claim of a national score.

    Fund data that can be reused

    Lead: NSF and NIST. Work with: researchers, nonprofit data stewards, vendors, and participating states.

    Issue open calls for task demonstrations and failure cases with machine-readable licenses, sensor details, hardware, site conditions, consent, and permitted uses. Compare formats with DROID, Open X-Embodiment, and LeRobot. Hold out independent evaluation sets. Release sensitive worker or facility data only where rights and security review allow.

    First decision: publish a data-rights checklist and two independent evaluations.

    Use mission labs for hard manipulation

    Lead: DOE and NSF. Work with: national labs, universities, and equipment suppliers.

    Use DOE's Genesis Mission collaboration and existing research awards for automated experiments, inspection in hazardous settings, and manipulation of deformable or irregular materials. Each award should specify the task, accessible test hardware, baseline, and a path for independent replication. NSTM-5 can guide the next budget request; new funding still requires the normal budget process.

    2. Industrial base

    Map production by part and plant

    Lead: Commerce and NIST. Work with: DOE, defense buyers, state MEP centers, robot makers, and tier suppliers.

    Measure U.S. and allied output, lead times, cost, and alternative designs for precision reducers, servo drives, motors, bearings, sensors, power electronics, and magnets. Split strain-wave reducers used in many cobots from RV reducers used in heavier arms. FAI reports spare Japanese reducer capacity, U.S. production in Massachusetts and Michigan, and possible dysprosium exposure in robot motors. Confirm utilization, material needs, and qualifying supply before proposing new factories or subsidies. Publish aggregate findings and protect firm-sensitive data.

    Build the service network beside the hardware

    Lead: NIST MEP and Commerce. Work with: community colleges, integrators, manufacturers, machine shops, and states.

    FAI estimates that integration services account for roughly two-thirds of deployment cost and identifies nearby machine shops as useful for fast prototypes. Test regional centers that pair a parts supplier, a system integrator, machine shops, and technicians who can maintain several robot brands. Give small manufacturers comparable quotes for total installed cost, prototype lead time, and service response. Use existing MEP automation work and track downtime after installation.

    Qualify screened allied suppliers

    Lead: Department of State, Commerce, and OSTP. Work with: UK, Japanese, and Korean agencies, standards bodies, and firms.

    The signed UK and Japan–Korea technology deals offer channels for joint tests and supply-chain work. Japan is a strong starting point for industrial arms and reducers; the UK for shared metrology and automated science; Korea for advanced manufacturing and electronics. Scope each project after checking beneficial ownership, component origin, export controls, and data access for China, Russia, and North Korea links. FAI notes that German-headquartered KUKA is Chinese-owned. Supplier review must reach beyond headquarters.

    3. Deployment at scale

    Make small-plant adoption measurable

    Lead: NIST MEP. Work with: state centers, manufacturers, SBA, and community colleges.

    September 2026 MEP awards provide more than $30 million in first-year support to 12 centers for AI, robotics, automation, and additive manufacturing, with at least 50% nonfederal match. Let centers use one protected template for installed cost, output, uptime, operator time, injury and near-miss reports, and payback. Compare adopters with eligible firms that did not buy. Link aggregate results to Census ASM robot measures. The existing bonus-depreciation rule may help eligible buyers finance equipment; it is a tax deduction, not a grant.

    Buy a result after a successful trial

    Lead: DOE, USDA, and federal facility owners. Work with: labs, operators, firms, and universities.

    Choose bounded tasks with a named customer: hazardous inspection, laboratory handling, farm packing, or facility logistics. Before a trial, publish the task, site safety case, baseline labor and operating cost, data rights, and purchase criteria. If the robot meets the criteria, the owner can compete a follow-on purchase under its procurement rules. A trial without a repeat buyer remains a research result.

    Train workers for installation and repair

    Lead: DOL and state workforce agencies. Work with: employers, unions, MEP centers, and colleges.

    Use local programs for controls, machine guarding, maintenance, and fault recovery. States can pair this with existing, eligibility-specific tools such as New York Excelsior, Michigan's general R&D credit, or California Competes. Track placements, wages, retention, and safety skills rather than promised jobs.

    4. Regulatory policies

    Specify evidence for workplace robot safety

    Lead: OSHA and NIST. Work with: employers, workers, insurers, and standards bodies.

    OSHA's robotics manual calls for site-specific hazard assessment across industrial, collaborative, and mobile systems. Publish worked examples for changing work cells, human proximity, emergency stops, and remote software updates. NIST test results can support those examples; they do not replace employer duties or site inspection. Separate voluntary test methods from binding requirements in every publication.

    Review rules that assume a human operator

    Lead: OSTP with OSHA, MSHA, FAA, and NHTSA. Work with: states, operators, workers, and safety researchers.

    Invite evidence on specific barriers to autonomous construction equipment, mine vehicles, drones, and road vehicles. FAI's 2025 OSTP filing proposes safety cases, named supervisors, data logs, and performance criteria; those are stakeholder proposals. Each regulator should publish the applicable rule, available waiver or interpretation path, decision time, and what would require notice-and-comment or Congress. Begin with a small set of applications and publish incident and intervention data.

    Clarify sourcing and security decisions

    Lead: Commerce and FCC, with federal buyers. Work with: suppliers and state purchasing offices.

    Commerce lists a robotics and industrial-machinery Section 232 investigation; buyers should not treat the investigation itself as a tariff. The FCC's Covered List action has a conditional-approval route for specified devices. Publish product-level FAQs and a component-provenance template so buyers can make lawful, documented choices without treating a headquarters address as proof of origin or security.
