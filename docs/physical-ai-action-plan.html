    Physical AI
    Action Plan

    A 24-month proposal to qualify robots for hazardous inspection, energy-equipment production, and laboratory handling, with repeat purchases as the test.

    Research draft · Revised 24 September 2026 · Prepared for the Robotics Leadership Tracker

    Start with three buying decisions

    The 2025 AI Action Plan organizes agency actions by pillar. The 2026 National Security Science and Technology Strategy asks for faster research, stronger production, and work with allies. Science: A New Golden Age (NSTM-5) sets the FY2028 federal R&D budget frame. NSPM-11 addresses AI used for national security. Civilian robotics actions below need their own agency authority and funding.

    The market case is specific. NSF/Census data show autonomous-systems and robotics use in 33% of firms with at least 25,000 employees, versus about 4% of firms with 1–9 employees; that survey category is broader than industrial robots. IFR reports a preliminary 38,000 U.S. industrial-robot installations in 2025, up 11%; installations say little about uptime or payback. NSF/Census · IFR.

    The Foundation for American Innovation (FAI) estimates that Japanese- and European-headquartered firms earn about 85% of traditional industrial-robot revenue. Its analysis also finds faster Chinese growth in collaborative robots and substantial U.S. reliance on imported arms. These are FAI estimates. U.S. agencies could test American AI controls on screened allied arms while qualifying domestic parts and local installers.

    1. Inspection: can an asset owner accept a tank or pipe inspection with the required defect detection and less hazardous exposure, including retrieval when the robot fails?
    2. Production: can an energy-equipment supplier produce accepted parts at lower total cost after changeovers, rework, integration, and maintenance?
    3. Laboratories: can a mission lab process samples faster while preserving traceability, contamination limits, and reproducibility?

    Proposed first cohort: two consenting sites per mission, six sites total, selected through an open process after the Prime Mover RFI. This is a manageable comparison design, not a national estimate. Use conventional automation as a comparator; fund learned control only where adaptation adds value. Every site needs a buyer, a baseline, an independent evaluator, and a priced route to continued operation.

    Four workstreams: test the task; qualify parts and service; earn a repeat purchase; resolve the approval path. All cohort sizes, deadlines, and gates below are proposals measured from authorization, not government commitments. Expand only after the second site reproduces the result.

    Likely direction, and the work still missing

    Estimate: more models will combine language, vision, and action across different robot bodies. Work on compliant mechanisms and human–robot interaction will also continue. The limiting evidence will be performance on unfamiliar parts, contact-rich tasks, changing work cells, and failures after deployment. A 2025 robotics research roadmap calls for diverse current data, transfer across platforms, lifelong learning, and safe use. A tactile manipulation review identifies hard contact skills that remain scarce in autonomous use.

    R&D priority: fund force and tactile sensing, deformation and insertion tasks, fault recovery, hardware transfer, and tests of human intervention. Measure durability and repair cost for new grippers and soft systems. Compare learned methods with conventional automation on the same task. Require independent trials across sites and robot types before funding scale-up. More papers alone do not establish reliability, cost, or worker benefit.

    1. Research and development

    R1. Publish three task protocols by day 90

    Accountable lead: NIST Engineering Laboratory; NSF supports research, with DOE site engineers, manufacturers, and worker representatives.

    Build on NIST's proposed baseline benchmark, but evaluate the job independently of robot shape. Inspection tests use seeded defects and a failed-robot retrieval exercise. Production tests use withheld part variants and changed fixtures. Laboratory tests use nonhazardous surrogate samples and deliberate labeling faults. Record attempts, accepted results, interventions, recovery time, and all operator support. Compare learned control with conventional automation on the same task.

    Deliverable / gate: by month 6, two independent facilities run each selected protocol. Publish disagreement and measurement uncertainty. If evaluators cannot reproduce the result, revise the test before using it for purchases.

    R2. Purchase reusable failure data

    Accountable lead: NSF; NIST defines the measurement fields. Labs, universities, and nonprofit stewards hold data under agreed rights.

    Fund missing examples from R1: slipping grips, failed insertions, obscured defects, interrupted communications, and human recovery. Use existing structures such as DROID, Open X-Embodiment, and LeRobot where suitable. Require calibration, task identifiers, attempt denominators, worker-recording consent, and licenses covering intended reuse. Keep held-out site data out of training.

    Deliverable / gate: by month 9, release a documented dataset or approved controlled-access route. Continue funding only when an outside team can use it to evaluate the target failure; recording hours alone do not qualify.

    R3. Make each mission award resolve one failure

    Accountable lead: DOE Environmental Management for cleanup tasks; Office of Science for laboratory tasks, subject to office agreement and mission authority.

    Start from operator needs in EM's technology work. DOE's documented Hanford inspection application provides a concrete precedent, not proof that every inspection is ready. Each proposed award names one unresolved failure, a 12-month target, a site engineer who will accept the result, and a replication site.

    Deliverable / gate: a costed test package by month 6. Stop or rescope when the target fails independent testing. Use existing facilities first; propose new infrastructure only with documented access or capability gaps.

    2. Industrial base

    I1. Qualify alternatives for the parts that stop work

    Accountable lead: Commerce, with NIST, DOE mission buyers, suppliers, and state manufacturing offices.

    By month 6, derive a component register from the six proposed sites: part function, manufacturing origin, ownership, qualified substitutes, lead time, annual demand, and days of spares. Separate strain-wave and RV reducers, motors and magnets, drives, sensors, and compute. FAI's industry analysis motivates checking capacity and integration costs; it does not establish a shortage for a particular buyer. Test a 90-day loss of each critical supplier.

    Deliverable / gate: by month 12, compare a qualified second source, additional spares, and a redesign for each material exposure. Recommend new capacity only where qualification fails or verified demand exceeds available supply; publish aggregate findings, not sensitive bills of materials.

    I2. Contract for service after installation

    Accountable lead: NIST MEP, with state centers, integrators, machine shops, and community colleges.

    Use MEP's automation assistance to request comparable bids for tooling, installation, qualification, training, maintenance, and recovery. Each production trial needs a named local service provider, spare-parts plan, response-time commitment, and software/data handover terms. Record travel, programming, and rework hours so hardware price does not conceal deployment cost.

    Deliverable / gate: by month 9, publish a redacted installed-cost breakdown and service agreement template. Before expansion, a local technician must demonstrate safe fault recovery without the vendor's development team on site.

    I3. Test allied substitution at the component level

    Accountable lead: Commerce; State coordinates willing foreign partners and NIST supports common tests.

    Use the UK and Japan–Korea technology arrangements as possible channels, not supplier approvals. Invite a second-source test for a part in I1. Check beneficial ownership, manufacturing location, subcomponents, remote access, and China, Russia, or North Korea links before selecting partners.

    Deliverable / gate: by month 12, publish one substitution test per selected critical part, including redesign and qualification cost. Record unresolved origin or access risks; allied headquarters alone cannot close the review.

    3. Deployment at scale

    D1. Separate task readiness from financing

    Lead: NIST MEP and SBA. Work with: state centers, lenders, manufacturers, and colleges.

    The 2022 Economic Census experimental series reports robot presence at 6.4% of manufacturing establishments, which employed 25.7% of manufacturing workers. Its definition includes active robots, purchases, or capital spending. The 2023 Annual Business Survey instead measures firms and found 1.3% using robotics across all industries in 2020–22. Do not mix the denominators. In MEP's 2026 awards, separate firms with a tested task but a financing gap from firms still unsure that a robot fits. For the second group, compare a robot with simpler automation and no purchase; a 2024 journal framework weighs cost, productivity, safety, and ergonomics.

    By day 90: MEP gives each candidate a written disposition: commercial purchase, qualification trial, research, or no robot. Log the binding constraint and the customer who can authorize a purchase; a vendor referral alone does not qualify.

    D2. Finance accepted work, including integration

    Lead: SBA, USDA Rural Development, and Treasury. Work with: MEP centers, state SSBCI managers, lenders, integrators, and insurers.

    For firms with a validated task, publish a lender template covering equipment, integration, maintenance, downtime, and operator training. Clarify how those costs fit existing SBA 7(a), eligible long-life equipment under SBA 504, rural USDA B&I, and state SSBCI terms. Eligibility varies by program and state. Compare defaults, downtime, and repeat purchases before proposing a new guarantee. Ask insurers what task and incident data would support underwriting; do not assume an insurance product exists.

    By month 6: obtain lender-reviewed cost templates and written eligibility determinations for proposed borrowers. Stress-test half the expected utilization and twice the expected recovery time. No new guarantee proposal without evidence of a credit gap among technically qualified buyers.

    D3. Require a second-site purchase decision

    Accountable lead: each DOE or private host's purchasing official, with an independent evaluator and site safety lead.

    Before installation, sign a trial charter: work envelope, accepted-output definition, maximum cost, minimum throughput, intervention limit, incident stop conditions, and operating budget. Run a proposed 90-day observation window after commissioning; record commissioning separately. Publish failed and withdrawn trials alongside successes. Safety approval cannot be traded for better economics.

    By month 18: issue a buy, retest, or stop decision at each site. By month 24, report competitively obtained follow-on purchases where applicable and second-site results. A successful trial creates no entitlement to a sole-source contract.

    D4. Pay for demonstrated maintenance skills

    Accountable lead: state workforce agencies with DOL, employers, unions, and colleges.

    Before commissioning, train the operators and technicians assigned to each shift in isolation, guarding, recovery, inspection, and update rollback. Budget paid training and shift coverage in D3. Workers help define stop conditions. Track reassignment, layoffs, contractors, and remote operators separately; report wages and retention six months after training.

    Gate: site staff must pass a practical recovery exercise before routine operation. Stop expansion if safe staffing or service coverage cannot be maintained.

    4. Regulatory policies

    P1. Publish a site acceptance dossier

    Lead: OSHA and NIST. Work with: employers, workers, insurers, and standards bodies.

    OSHA's robotics manual calls for site-specific hazard assessment across industrial, collaborative, and mobile systems. Publish worked examples for changing work cells, human proximity, emergency stops, and remote software updates. Record model and software versions, retest after material changes, and give site owners a safe rollback path. NIST's manufacturing cybersecurity work already includes change control. A Safety Science study finds training and organizational readiness matter alongside machine risk. Voluntary test methods do not replace employer duties or site inspection.

    By month 6: publish worked dossiers for the three missions: hazard assessment, applicable requirements, operating limits, recovery procedure, update retest triggers, and approval signatures. Unresolved safety findings block field operation; the responsible site authority retains approval.

    P2. Resolve named rules, one task at a time

    Lead: OSTP with OSHA, MSHA, FAA, and NHTSA. Work with: states, operators, workers, and safety researchers.

    Invite evidence on specific barriers to autonomous construction equipment, mine vehicles, drones, and road vehicles. FAI's 2025 OSTP filing proposes safety cases, named supervisors, data logs, and performance criteria; those are stakeholder proposals. Each regulator should publish the applicable rule, available waiver or interpretation path, decision time, and what would require notice-and-comment or Congress. A 2025 policy paper proposes moving from test zones and living labs to policy evidence; a 2024 study questions treating simulation alone as proof of safety. Begin with bounded applications and report field incidents and interventions.

    Within 90 days of RFI close: publish a referral register with rule citation, regulator, requested remedy, required evidence, and target response date. A referral is not a waiver. Defer trials outside an approved operating envelope.

    P3. Review each product and update path

    Lead: Commerce and FCC, with federal buyers. Work with: suppliers and state purchasing offices.

    Commerce lists a robotics and industrial-machinery Section 232 investigation; buyers should not treat the investigation itself as a tariff. The FCC's Covered List action has a conditional-approval route for specified devices. Publish product-level FAQs and a component-provenance template so buyers can make lawful, documented choices without treating a headquarters address as proof of origin or security.

    Before purchase: the buyer records the applicable restriction and effective date, supported software life, remote-access owner, disconnected operating mode, and tested rollback. Recheck after material hardware or software changes.

    First 24 months

    Authorize a bounded comparison before promising national scale. OSTP coordinates proposed leads; OMB and agency counsel check mission fit, available funds, and procurement paths. Agencies retain their own decisions.

    Cost the six-site proposal before funding it

    Each host estimates equipment and tooling, integration, qualification, independent evaluation, paid training, 12 months of service, removal, and contingency. Separate federal cost, host contribution, and commercial financing. Publish an aggregate range after responses arrive; no defensible national appropriation follows from robot sticker prices alone.

    Existing awards or facilities may support eligible work after agency review. New grants, guarantees, or capacity subsidies require their own authority and funding. NSTM-5 can inform a budget proposal; it is not an appropriation. Escalate unfunded work through the budget process rather than implying that coordination creates spending power.

    Publish the denominator, including failure

    Report sites screened, accepted, withdrawn, failed, and purchasing again. For each mission report attempts and accepted results, operating and downtime hours, interventions per 100 operating hours, cost per accepted unit, worker exposure, and staffing changes. Separate commissioning from routine operation and vendor-assisted work from independent operation. Six selected sites cannot establish national adoption or causal job effects.
