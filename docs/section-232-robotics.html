    Section 232 action on robotics and industrial machinery

    The October 28, 2026 proclamation with each clause explained, the statutes it cites, its dates, the officials who carry it out, and the companies exposed. Taken from the proclamation and the White House fact sheet. Checked October 29, 2026.

    Bottom line

    No tariff is imposed. The President agreed with Commerce that imports of robots, industrial machinery and their parts threaten to impair national security, and chose negotiation first. Commerce and the U.S. Trade Representative must report on the talks by April 26, 2027. Tariffs, quotas, fees and an investment-incentive program are named as options for later. Checked October 29, 2026.

    The action in brief

    • Finding. The President agrees with the Commerce Secretary. In the proclamation's words, robotics, industrial machinery and their parts "are being imported into the United States in such quantities and under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security" (para. 9).
    • Remedy chosen. Negotiation. Commerce and USTR "shall jointly pursue or continue pursuing negotiations of agreements" with any trading partner they deem appropriate (para. 10, clause 1). No country, product list or tariff rate is named (para. 10).
    • Options reserved. Depending on the talks, the President "may consider" tariffs, duties, quotas and fees, and "establishing a program to incentivize new investment in United States production" of robots, machinery and parts (clause 1).
    • Reporting. Commerce and USTR update the President "from time to time", with one update within 180 days (clause 2). Commerce keeps monitoring imports and flags anything that may call for more action (clause 3).
    • Findings behind it. The U.S. "relies on foreign imports for most types" of robots and machinery and "produces less robotics and industrial equipment than it consumes." Equipment from "certain foreign entities" carries cybersecurity risk (paras. 5 to 7). No country is named.

    The fact sheet

    The fact sheet restates the directive to negotiate agreements on imports "from any country" and says the Secretary "will inform the President of any circumstances that might indicate the need for further action." It places the action in a run of 2025 and 2026 Section 232 measures on steel, aluminum, copper, automobiles, trucks, timber and lumber, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, polysilicon and drones. It gives no figures beyond the proclamation's.

    The proclamation's clauses

    Each paragraph of the proclamation and what it means
    Para.TextReading
    1Within the past 90 days, the Commerce Secretary sent the President a Section 232 report finding that these imports threaten to impair national security.The investigation is finished and the finding is affirmative. The report itself has not been published.
    2Nearly all defense systems and critical infrastructure depend on robots or industrial machinery. Cites about $7.1 trillion in 2024 manufactured-goods shipments and "approximately $86 billion worth of equipment in 2024."Sets the scale and the defense link. The proclamation does not say what the $86 billion measures.
    3Robots and machinery build aircraft, ships, munitions and missiles; "humanoid and quadruped robots, and autonomous ground vehicles" support the military.Names humanoid and quadruped robots among the defense uses, and ties wartime surge production to industrial robots.
    4Robots and machines make products used across all 16 critical-infrastructure sectors; heavy use in automotive, electrical and electronics.Names the main buying industries.
    5The U.S. "relies on foreign imports for most types" of robots, machinery and parts, and imports are rising.Import dependence is the core finding. No country shares are given.
    6Equipment from "certain foreign entities" poses security and safety risks because it processes and stores sensitive data.A finding only. It names no country or company and restricts nothing by itself.
    7Domestic output falls short of national-security needs; U.S. capacity is underused despite recent factory investment.The rationale for an investment program as well as border measures.
    8 to 9The Secretary recommended actions; the President concurs and decides to negotiate.The page reads this as the President's determination under 1862(c)(1)(A); the proclamation does not cite that subsection. The recommendations are not public.
    10Commerce and USTR negotiate with partners they choose and update within 180 days.Sets the only date in the text.
    11Paraphrases 1862(c)(3)(A): if no agreement within 180 days, or it is not carried out or is ineffective, the President may take other actions, including tariffs, duties, quotas and fees.Uses "may" where the statute says "shall." Federal Circuit rulings allow later steps under a plan adopted on time.
    12Section 604 of the Trade Act lets the President write such actions into the Harmonized Tariff Schedule.The legal route for any future duty to reach the tariff schedule.
    Clause 1Negotiate; alternative remedies later, including tariffs, duties, quotas, fees, and an investment-incentive program.Recent tariff proclamations on polysilicon and drones built such programs as duty waivers for companies building U.S. plants.
    Clause 2Updates from time to time; one within 180 days.April 26, 2027.
    Clause 3Commerce monitors imports and tells the President of any need for further action.Commerce can bring the matter back before the 180 days run out.
    Clause 4Inconsistent provisions of earlier proclamations are superseded; severability.Standard. It does not touch the FCC or Treasury robot measures, which rest on other laws.

    The statutes

    The laws the proclamation cites, what each one does, and the cases that limit them

    Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 (19 U.S.C. 1862)

    • (b) Investigation. Commerce investigates, consults the Defense Department, and must report "no later than" 270 days after starting, with recommendations "for action or inaction."
    • (c)(1) Decision. Within 90 days of the report the President decides whether he agrees and, if so, "the nature and duration of the action." Any action is to be implemented within 15 days. No court decision on whether a decision to negotiate has anything to implement was found.
    • (c)(2) Congress. Within 30 days the President sends Congress a written statement of the reasons.
    • (c)(3)(A) The negotiation route. This applies when the action is "the negotiation of an agreement which limits or restricts the importation" of the article. If none is reached within 180 days, or an agreement "is not being carried out or is ineffective," the President "shall take such other actions as the President deems necessary." Those actions must be published in the Federal Register. The proclamation uses "may" for the same step.
    • (d) Factors. Commerce and the President weigh domestic capacity, imports and their effect on industry, and "the close relation of the economic welfare of the Nation to our national security."

    Section 604 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. 2483)

    Lets the President "embody in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule" the substance of trade actions. It is how a future duty would be written into the tariff schedule.

    3 U.S.C. 301 (text)

    Lets the President delegate functions to agency heads, in writing and published in the Federal Register. Cited in the preamble, as in every 2026 Section 232 proclamation.

    Cases and precedents

    • Autos, 2019 to 2025. Proclamation 9888 (May 2019) also chose negotiation, with a 180-day update. Tariffs came in Proclamation 10908 (March 2025), which said the talks "did not lead to any agreements." Among the 2019 to 2026 actions reviewed here, it is the only negotiate-first case whose outcome is known.
    • 2026 negotiate-first actions. Critical minerals (Proc. 11001, January), commercial aircraft (Proc. 11040, July) and wind turbines (Proc. 11074, October) use the same structure. No public outcome of the critical-minerals update, due July 13, 2026, has been found.
    • Transpacific Steel v. United States (Fed. Cir. 2021). A President who announces a continuing plan of action on time may add to it later.
    • PrimeSource v. United States (Fed. Cir. 2023). "There is no textual basis for a specific time limit on adjustments under a timely adopted plan." Derivative products can be added without a separate report. The court reserved the case where the finding has become "substantively stale."
    • Learning Resources v. Trump (Supreme Court, Feb 20, 2026). Held 6 to 3 that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs. Section 232 was not at issue, though the opinion describes it as a statute that authorizes duties.

    Paths the text allows

    The routes left open, with the U.S. plant capacity already announced
    • Talks continue past April 26, 2027. The autos case ran this way: negotiations opened in 2019 and tariffs came in 2025. The statute sets no deadline for the later action, and the Federal Circuit has upheld later additions under a plan adopted on time.
    • Agreements with ceilings. The drone action capped duties at 15% for six allied partners, and the EU has asked for a 15% ceiling here. No published framework applying one to robots was found.
    • Tariffs on finished goods, not parts. This is what Boston Dynamics, Agility and Haas asked for. Boston Dynamics and ABB warned that tariffs on parts raise U.S. makers' costs.
    • An investment program. The text reserves one; the drone proclamation, by contrast, said the Secretary "shall establish" its program. The 2026 template waives duties for companies building U.S. plants on a deadline.
    • Security measures on specific makers. Paragraph 6 is a finding and sets no measure. Instruments that already address specific makers include the FCC Covered List, a Commerce supply-chain rule and procurement law.

    Open questions

    What the text and the record leave unsettled
    • When Commerce reported. The report was due May 30, 2026. "Within the past 90 days" puts it on or after July 30. The same wording appears in the aircraft, drone, polysilicon and wind-turbine proclamations. No ruling on a late report was found.
    • "May" or "shall." After 180 days without an agreement the statute says the President "shall take such other actions"; the proclamation says he "may." The statute also describes an agreement that "limits or restricts the importation"; the proclamation directs agreements "to address the threatened impairment."
    • Scope. The proclamation does not define "robotics" or "industrial machinery" and lists no tariff codes. The FCC's definition (mobile robots only) and Treasury's (AI-enabled examples) are different instruments and do not set this one's scope.
    • Partners. No country is named for the talks. No published trade framework with the EU, Japan or Korea that caps a robotics rate was found.
    • The report. The statute calls for publishing the unclassified parts of Commerce's report (1862(b)(3)(B)); it has not been published.
    • Reaction. No law-firm alert, trade-association statement or market move tied to the proclamation was found as of Oct 29.
    • Federal Register. No proclamation number or citation yet. This page will add them when published.
